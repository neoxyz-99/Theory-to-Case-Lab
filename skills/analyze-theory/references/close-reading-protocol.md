## 1. Corpus pass

Inspect the table of contents, preface or introduction, chapter openings and conclusions, index, bibliography, diagrams, and notes. Identify where the theory is formulated, qualified, applied, revised, or defended. Do not assume the most quoted chapter contains the complete theory.

For a book, create a chapter-role map:

| Chapter/section | Function | Core concepts | Claims/cases | Importance |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## 2. Argument pass

For every major argument, extract:

- problem or target;
- conclusion;
- stated reasons or premises;
- implicit bridge premises;
- evidence or illustration;
- objection addressed;
- qualification and scope;
- unresolved inference.

Distinguish the order of exposition from the reconstructed order of justification. Label bridge premises added by the analyst `[A]`.

## 3. Concept pass

Create a concept ledger:

| Concept | Source definition | Working interpretation | Relation to other concepts | Ambiguity/change | Locator |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Check whether a term is descriptive, explanatory, evaluative, methodological, or metaphorical. Track shifts in meaning across chapters, periods, translations, or later editions.

## 4. Case pass

Do not merely summarize examples. Classify the function of each case:

- motivates the problem;
- illustrates a concept;
- demonstrates a mechanism;
- supplies empirical support;
- offers a paradigm or ideal type;
- works as a counterexample;
- establishes a boundary condition;
- persuades rhetorically without testing the claim.

Use this table:

| Case | Outcome/phenomenon | Theory concept | Mechanism or interpretive step | Argumentative function | Evidential weight | Limits | Locator |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Never treat an illustration as proof merely because it is memorable.

## 5. Case-explanation pass

Do not explain a case by attaching concept labels to it. First identify the outcome, difference, practice, or meaning that requires explanation. Then show how the theory changes the analysis.

For causal or mechanism-oriented theories, reconstruct:

`outcome difference → theoretical position → resources and constraints → mechanism → changed choice set → outcome and distributional effects`

For interpretive, critical, genealogical, or normative theories, reconstruct:

`text/practice → conceptual location → interpretive operation → meaning or power relation revealed → alternative reading → boundary`

For either path, state the unit and level of analysis, evidence required, rival explanation or reading, counterfactual or negative case when appropriate, and scope conditions. Preserve the distinction between the source's own case `[P]` and a new application proposed by the analyst `[A]`.

## 6. Reconstruction pass

Express the theory in five layers:

1. ontology: what kinds of entities or relations the theory assumes;
2. unit and level of analysis;
3. concepts and their relations;
4. mechanism, interpretive sequence, or causal pathway;
5. outcomes, implications, and scope conditions.

When suitable, use this compact schema:

`context/scope → assumptions → concepts/relations → mechanism or interpretive move → expected outcome/meaning → observable or textual implications`

Do not force causal notation onto interpretive, genealogical, critical, or normative theory. Adapt the schema to the theory's epistemic form.

When a diagram materially clarifies at least three relationships, follow [visual-expression-protocol.md](visual-expression-protocol.md). Explain its purpose and limit before showing it, distinguish source-explicit `[P]` links from analyst-reconstructed `[A]` links, and interpret the diagram in prose afterward.

## 7. Critical pass

First state the most charitable coherent version of the theory. Then test:

- internal coherence;
- definition stability;
- explanatory or interpretive gain;
- evidence fit;
- alternative explanations or readings;
- boundary cases;
- historical and cultural portability;
- normative and political commitments;
- methodological consequences.

Classify criticism as:

- `immanent`: uses the theory's own commitments;
- `empirical`: disputes evidence, prediction, or applicability;
- `conceptual`: disputes definitions or logical relations;
- `methodological`: disputes how claims can be supported;
- `external/normative`: judges by a competing value or framework;
- `reception-based`: criticizes how later users operationalized or simplified it.

Do not attribute a weakness in one study to the whole theory without an explicit bridge argument.

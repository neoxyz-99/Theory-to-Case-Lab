# Evidence and Citation Protocol

## Purpose

Keep theory interpretation traceable without pretending that all scholarly judgments are mechanical.

## Evidence classes

Use these labels in notes and evidence tables. They may be omitted from polished prose only when the distinction remains unmistakable.

| Label | Meaning | Acceptable support |
|---|---|---|
| `[P]` | Claim about the focal theorist or primary text | Supplied primary text or verified edition |
| `[S]` | Claim about scholarly interpretation, application, criticism, or evidence | Verified external scholarly source |
| `[A]` | Reconstruction, synthesis, evaluation, or proposed application | Reasoning grounded in `[P]` and/or `[S]`, explicitly identified |

Do not cite a secondary source as though it were the theorist's own formulation when the primary text is available. When only a secondary account is accessible, write “as interpreted by…” and flag the missing primary verification.

## Locator hierarchy

Use the most precise locator available:

1. page plus chapter or section;
2. chapter or section when editions paginate differently;
3. paragraph, figure, table, note, or stable anchor;
4. stable URL plus access date for web-only material.

Record the edition for books. Do not silently transfer page numbers between printings, translations, or electronic editions. For OCR-derived text, mark uncertain quotations and verify visually before quoting.

## Claim discipline

- Quote only wording whose formulation matters. Paraphrase routine content.
- Preserve modality: “may,” “tends to,” and “under these conditions” must not become universal claims.
- Preserve the population, period, setting, method, and outcome of empirical research.
- Treat abstracts and reviews as discovery aids; inspect the full text for central claims when possible.
- Mark inaccessible or partially inspected sources as `verification needed`.
- Never infer scholarly consensus from a few highly ranked results.

## Source selection for research landscapes

Build a balanced source set when available:

- primary/canonical formulation;
- authoritative histories or handbooks;
- influential extensions or revisions;
- representative applications across relevant domains;
- major critics and competing accounts;
- recent reviews and recent substantive studies;
- negative, null, limiting, or boundary-condition evidence.

Prefer peer-reviewed articles, academic books, chapters from reputable presses, dissertations when uniquely relevant, and authoritative indexes. Explain the use of preprints, blogs, encyclopedia entries, or teaching materials.

## Bibliographic verification

Before listing a reference, verify at least title, author, year, and publication venue. Verify DOI or ISBN when supplied. If full bibliographic details cannot be verified, place the item in “Leads to verify,” not the final bibliography.

## Search reporting

For claims about current research, record:

- search date;
- databases, indexes, websites, or search tools used;
- principal query families;
- language and date limits;
- inclusion logic;
- important access limitations.

Use “the searched literature suggests” when the corpus does not justify a field-wide claim.

## Citation and writing safety

Do not fabricate citations to make a literature review look complete. Do not cite a source for a claim it merely mentions. Separate the source's conclusion from the agent's synthesis. Identify any proposed paragraph that still requires the user to check the original source.
